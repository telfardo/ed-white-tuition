The violin

Playing, and passing it on

Twenty years with the instrument — orchestras, European tours, a jazz group and world music concerts. The best bit is still watching someone else find their sound.

On stage

Ten years in orchestras, and still gigging

Ed was taught by the lead violinist of the award-winning Liverpool Philharmonic, and spent ten years in orchestras — including touring Europe. These days he curates world music concerts as a solo artist, drawing on Indian classical, flamenco and folk, and co-leads a jazz group in Edinburgh.

It matters for teaching. Everything he asks a student to do, he's had to do himself — under pressure, in front of people, on a night when it wasn't going well.

Youngest students

Six years old, first size violin

Small hands, short attention spans, enormous enthusiasm. Lessons with young beginners are built around games, silly noises and very short wins — a good note, a whole bar, a tune their family recognises.

Nobody gets told off for squeaking. Squeaking is part of it.

Ed with one of his youngest violin students.

Never too late

Seventy-five, and playing for the first time

Plenty of Ed's students come to the violin late — some picking it up for the very first time in retirement, others returning to an instrument they put down forty years ago. There's no age at which this stops being possible.

The pace is different, the goals are different, and the pleasure is exactly the same.

A weekly lesson that's become one of Ed's favourites.

However you learn best

Grades, orchestra parts, or entirely by ear

Working through a grade syllabus? Ed will take you through it. Struggling with an orchestra part before the next rehearsal? Bring it along.

But he's an intuitive player, and a firm believer in training your ear so you can pick up a tune just by listening. Improvisation, world music, folk — whatever takes your fancy is fair game.
